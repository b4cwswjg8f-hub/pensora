US Citizens in Germany: Avoiding the PFIC Trap

If you're a US citizen or green card holder, the "just buy a broad ETF" advice that works for everyone else can be a serious tax mistake for you specifically.

The short version: most ordinary European ETFs — including the exact kind of broad, low-cost index funds recommended everywhere else on this site — are classified by the US as Passive Foreign Investment Companies (PFICs). For US persons, that classification triggers punitive default taxation and burdensome annual reporting. This is one of the most common, costly mistakes American expats make in Germany.

Why this only hits Americans

The United States is one of the only countries in the world that taxes based on citizenship, not residency. As a US citizen or green card holder, you remain a US taxpayer on your worldwide income no matter where you live — including years spent entirely in Germany, paying German tax, with no US income at all.

The PFIC problem

Nearly all non-US domiciled investment funds — including the UCITS ETFs that are the standard, sensible default for everyone else living in Germany — are classified as PFICs under US tax law. Without specific elections (which have their own complexity and cost), the default PFIC tax treatment can mean gains taxed at the top US ordinary rate, plus an interest charge calculated as if the tax had been due incrementally since the day you invested. On top of that, each PFIC fund generally requires its own annual Form 8621 filing.

What to do instead

Most US-person expats in Germany avoid this entirely by investing in US-domiciled ETFs and mutual funds, or holding individual stocks directly, rather than European funds — sidestepping PFIC classification altogether. Whether this is accessible to you depends on which brokers you can use from Germany, since some German and EU brokers don't offer US-domiciled products, and some brokers restrict US-person accounts entirely due to FATCA compliance costs.

FATCA and broker access

The Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act requires foreign banks and brokers to report US account holders to US authorities. The compliance burden this creates is exactly why some German and European brokers decline US-person clients, or limit what they can buy — worth checking before you open an account, not after.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a PFIC?
A Passive Foreign Investment Company (PFIC) is a US tax classification that applies to almost all non-US domiciled ETFs and mutual funds — including ordinary European UCITS ETFs. If you're a US person, buying these can trigger punitive US tax and reporting rules.
Why are PFICs so costly for US citizens?
Under the default PFIC tax regime, gains can be taxed at the highest US ordinary income rate, with an interest charge applied as if tax had been owed since the investment was made. Annual Form 8621 filing is also required per fund.
Who counts as a US person for this purpose?
US citizens and green card holders are taxed by the US on worldwide income regardless of where they live — citizenship-based taxation. This obligation follows you to Germany and applies to European fund purchases too.
What should US citizens in Germany invest in instead?
Most stick to US-domiciled ETFs and mutual funds or direct individual stock holdings, both of which avoid PFIC classification. The right structure depends on your specific broker access — worth reviewing individually.
Does FATCA affect me too?
Yes. FATCA requires foreign financial institutions, including German banks and brokers, to report US persons' account information to US authorities — and some German brokers restrict or decline US-person clients because of it.
